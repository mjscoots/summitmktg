## How the sale is built

Every door follows the same road map. Memorize the skeleton first. Personality comes after. Rookies who start riffing too early lose deals.

Intro, then Bridge, then Price Sheet, then Objections, then Close, then Paperwork.

The five customer types on that road map:

- Fresh account: no service yet. Bridge, sheet, close.
- Not interested: stay neutral, ask when they last sprayed, get back on the route.
- Switchover: who are they with, how often, then show the difference. Do not bash the other company.
- DIY: start from what they already use, then show the depth difference.
- Renter: treat it as a real type, then map the conversation. Do not invent a discount on this page.

## 1. Why you're here, and the mental game

Sales is a persuasive conversation about value transfer. You already do this. You ask for the extension, you pick the restaurant, you get buy-in. The door is the same skill, run on purpose.

Everyone is working for some greater purpose. Become the person worthy of it. Write it down so it is real.

The mental game comes before the script.

- Neutrality: elite reps live there. Do not ride a good door. Do not spiral after a bad one. Reset every single door.
- The mindset stack: 1% better (win this door, not the whole summer). No commission breath. Neutral equals now. Consistency beats feelings. Pressure is a privilege.
- Five discipline rules: show up regardless, someone is always better so learn from them, no negativity in the car group, discipline now or regret later, every action is a vote for who you want to become.
- Commit to the input, not the outcome you cannot control. Role play tonight. Talk to people. Stay out. Do not write a number you cannot control and call it a commitment.

From the Rookie Manual, the five principles for a successful summer: commit to consistency and a strict schedule, be obsessed with learning, step outside your comfort zone, stay hungry and remember your purpose, have fun and be positive.

## 2. The six laws of the sale

Master these six. Every system in door-to-door fits underneath them.

1. Questions. The prospect sells themselves.
2. Pain. Stay in discovery longer than feels comfortable. Diagnose before you prescribe.
3. Certainty. People buy confidence before they buy the product. Certainty in you, the company, the service, and the timing.
4. Control. Lead every conversation to a decision. You control the path.
5. Momentum. Sales compound. Protect the buzz.
6. Activity. Most problems are activity problems in disguise.

Source: The Sales Blueprint FINAL, "The 6 Laws of the Sale" (local extract and Drive SalesBlueprint...., file id `1-WrtL-dHYarog1YmWZ1mfAN1fspa3pkbaAagqbTqyBs`). ## 3. The intro. Four parts, no exceptions.

1. Begin with a question. Interrupt the "how do I get rid of this person" loop. "Hey, how's it going?" or "Quick question for ya."
2. Justify your presence. Short and confident. Who you are and why you're on the porch.
3. Name two local pest issues. Neighborhood-specific. Relevance plus credibility. Get the nod: you know how it gets, right?
4. Lead to the golden question. Nod, palm open, pause long enough that they ask "How much?" Then mumble "I'll show ya" and roll into the bridge. Do not break the flow.

Non-verbals matter as much as the words. Distance, open palms, no fidgeting, eye contact, slow nods. A bad intro is almost always rushed, quiet, or unsure. Diagnose it, then run it again.

## 4. The bridge

The bridge sits between "How much?" and the price sheet. Its job is to kill objections before they show up, and to anchor value before they see a number.

Say what makes the service different from the monthly corporate model. First two visits back to back, to break the egg cycle, then less often after that. Ask square footage. Pull the sheet immediately. Do not break the flow.

If you skip the bridge, you walk a price with no context. That is how you get a price fight instead of a conversation.

## 5. The price sheet

The sheet is how you build both an emotional case (what they hear) and a logical case (what they read). Walk it. Do not recite it.

Goals of the sheet, from Luc's deck and the Rookie Manual:

1. Build value around the service, efficiently.
2. Shorten the sales cycle.
3. Save features and benefits as ammunition, instead of dumping them all at once.
4. Raise the quality of the agreement, not by talking louder.
5. Validate the pitch with something they can see.
6. Speak the customer's language.

What a good walk looks like: under two minutes, questions in it so it stays a conversation, tailored to the pest they just named. Point as you speak. Neutral, measured tone. Slow down. Subtle nods on the key lines.

Standard service first: foundation, inside and out, the everyday crawlers. Then premiums, tied to what they actually see. Every premium gets a feature and a benefit, not a label.

Examples of premiums you train (feature, then benefit):

- Mice and rats: bait boxes and seal obvious entry points, so they are not running around the house.
- Wasps and hornets: scrub the nests and treat so they do not come back, so nobody is getting stung.
- De-webbing: scrub the webs so the house looks clean, and treat so they do not rebuild on the same pheromone trail.
- Deep soil: granulate the yard so fleas, ticks, and ants get flushed, and the yard is as usable as the inside.

After the "for everything" line: stop. Own the silence. If they nod or say okay, go to paperwork. Do not keep talking.

The first price they see is the anchor. Never verbally contradict the sheet. If a price has to move later, give a real reason (route, yard, density). Never drop on the first objection. Build value first. The exact dollar sequence stays in the team decks, not on this page.

## 6. Question-based selling

The prospect sells themselves. You ask. You do not speech.

The 1-2-3 question formula is the drill. One question opens it. Two questions go deeper. Three questions find the real issue. Stay in pain longer than feels comfortable. Find the need behind the need.

Think like a doctor. Diagnose, then prescribe. Common miss: pitching the service before you know what actually bothers them. Everyone's favorite subject is themselves. Ask, then listen.

Early questions build rapport. Later questions find hot buttons (ants in the kitchen, webs they keep knocking down, pets in the yard). Badly timed questions feel like an interrogation. Time them.

Source: The Sales Blueprint FINAL, Law of Questions, Law of Pain, and the 1-2-3 Question Formula. Rookie Manual, Chapter 13, Question-Based Selling, and Chapter 14, Rapport Building (Drive `19x84KbvQle2MlS4zpcQJYc4XCzC6Y4V1qaN9EnfKCMk`).

## 7. Features and benefits

A feature is what you do. A benefit is what they get. Rookies name features and hope the customer translates. Do the translation for them.

"I granulate the whole yard" is a feature. "You can actually use the backyard" is the benefit. "I scrub the webs and treat the pheromones" is a feature. "They stop rebuilding in the same corner" is the benefit.

Ask what they are seeing, then pick the two premiums that match. Deliver each as feature, then benefit. That is also how a switchover sounds when you use "rather than": rather than just knocking webs down, you scrub and treat so they do not come back. Compare without bashing.

Source: Rookie Manual, Chapter 15, Features and Benefits, and Chapter 27, Create a Need, Give a Solution (Drive `19x84KbvQle2MlS4zpcQJYc4XCzC6Y4V1qaN9EnfKCMk`). 1 Basic Script, premium feature and benefit list (Drive `10Eym4OvNkPOzu1P-rEQoJb5whymdKvXcf-Pn9x0BXWE`). ## 8. Objections. Yes, Address, Close.

Questions are not objections. A question is information. An objection is a reason they have not bought yet. You want the real one. Soft closes surface it.

The formula, every time:

1. Yes. Neutralize it. "Yeah." "Totally." "Of course." Do not debate. Do not flinch.
2. Address. Show you heard them. Add a relevant feature or benefit.
3. Close. Pull one close from the arsenal. Then stop talking. Own the silence. Run the cycle again if you need to.

Most reps quit too early.

Common objection types you train (the rolls live in the objection doc, not here): renter, spouse, bad time, I do it myself, leave me a card, money, one-time spray, no bugs or not a big deal.

Body language and tonality carry this. If your face argues, the words do not matter.

## 9. The close

There are two categories. Soft closes first. Hard closes last, and only when they are ready to buy. You can only go hard so many times before they start to recognize the pattern and put a wall up. Run soft closes as many times as you need, within reason.

Soft closes make them give you the real objection. Hard closes take the agreement.

The close arsenal:

- Option: inside or just outside, text or email, 3 or 5, now or later this afternoon.
- Assignment: dogs in, cars out of the garage, sprinklers on after the soil treatment, side gate unlocked, toys and pet bowls moved. This is a close, not a chore list.
- Assumptive: you'd want the garage done too, right? The play set? The fence line?
- Statement: what's a good last name, phone, email. Let's knock this out while I'm here.
- Sincerity: saved for last. Give me a shot. I will not let you down.
- Fear of loss, from the Blueprint: limited spots left on today's route. Use it honestly, not as a trick.

Make flashcards. Front is the type. Back is three lines you can actually say.

The environmental close, also called the backyard pitch, is the one to master. It is used when they are in a non-buying state. Motion creates emotion. Break the no-zone, get them moving, find the real problem in the yard, build value on what you can both see, then close. Format is in the environmental doc. Write your own from that format. Do not read it like a monologue.

## 10. Switchover and DIY

If they already have a company, that is good news. They already believe in the service. Your job is not to convince them pests are real. Your job is to show why switching makes more sense.

Stay calm. Never act surprised. Ask who they use and how often. Let them explain what that company does. Then show what you do that is more, not just different. Use "rather than." Do not talk down the other company.

There is a preempted version (you ask who they use as part of the intro) and a non-preempted version (they volunteer it). Know both.

DIY is the same posture. "That's exactly why I'm here." Neighbors do it themselves too. Then show the depth difference and go to the sheet.

## 11. Certainty, body language, and first impressions

People buy certainty. You transmit it with tonality, body language, and eye contact, not with a longer pitch.

Low and slow is authority. Speak from your chest. Down-tone on statements. Speed sounds like a salesman. Slow sounds like a professional. Hold eye contact. Nod slowly. Forced breaks look weak. Open palms. Take up space. Zero fidgeting.

Be assumptive without being rude. You belong on that porch. Look the part, act the part. The tech approach is the same idea: you are the person who knows the house and the pests, not a stranger reading a card.

First impressions are short. Be likable, justify why you're there, then get to work. Do not spend the first thirty seconds apologizing for knocking.

## 12. Know the service

You cannot sell what you cannot explain. Learn what the work actually is.

- Power spray and the standard exterior and interior treatment.
- Granulation and yard treatments.
- Eave treatments and de-webbing.
- Entry points and spot interior work.
- How the approach changes with construction and garages.

Know the usual pests and what you do for each: roaches, spiders, centipedes and the other foundation crawlers, ants, wasps and hornets, mice, rats. Neighborhood clues tell you what to look for before you knock.

## 13. Momentum and the buzz

Sales compound. One sale makes the next one easier. Protect that.

The steps from the Blueprint and the Rookie Manual:

1. Visualize a full day of wins before you knock. Say it out loud.
2. Get the first sale early. Name-drop neighbors. Move-ins buy fastest. Look for signs of life.
3. Knock while the tech is spraying. Point to the truck. That is instant credibility. Pull people off the porch.
4. Do not break it. Momentum is oxygen. Stopping mid-buzz kills it.
5. Ride the wave. Head down. Do not look up and admire it.
6. Say thank you. Be humble. Gratitude multiplies it.

Source: The Sales Blueprint FINAL, Law of Momentum and "Building the Buzz. Step by Step." Rookie Manual, Chapter 31, First Summer, Discovering the Buzz (Drive `19x84KbvQle2MlS4zpcQJYc4XCzC6Y4V1qaN9EnfKCMk`).

## 14. Area management, go-backs, logging, referrals

Your area is a resource. Do not burn it. Work it on purpose.

Plan streets the night before. Circle the same chunk at different hours. Morning is for signs of life. Prime time is for finishing conversations. Track who the decision-maker is, what they care about, and when to come back. Notes matter.

The 15-second rule from the manual: knock twice, then ring, wait a short beat, and if no one answers, move on.

Go-backs are planned, not random. Know why you're going back and what you will say. Talking to a non-decision maker is not a close. Get a time when the right person is home.

Logging is how you see where you actually get stopped. Do not guess. Do not overload yourself with notes you will never read.

Referrals: ask before you leave. Commercial buildings and extra services come up the same way. Have a simple ask. Then do it every time.

Source: The Sales Blueprint FINAL, "Area Management. Work Smart." ## 15. Solidifying the sale and working with techs

The sale is not done when they say yes. Solidify it. Set expectations honestly. Do not oversell because you got nervous on the porch.

Customers should know what happens on the first visit, that they may see more activity after the first treatment (the product is flushing pests), and how to reach you if something comes up. Grateful, clear, no surprises.

Same-day techs have hard rules in the manual. Treat techs well. You are on the same job. Set realistic expectations with the customer. Do not promise a result the tech cannot deliver.

Source: Rookie Manual, Chapters 21 (Solidifying the Sale), 22 (Expectation Management), and 37 (Working with Same Day Technicians) (Drive `19x84KbvQle2MlS4zpcQJYc4XCzC6Y4V1qaN9EnfKCMk`). ## 16. Deliberate practice

Inspiration is not a plan. Deliberate practice is. Ten focused reps on one segment beat one sloppy walkthrough of the whole pitch.

Role-play rotations from the Blueprint:

1. Intro to the golden question. Goal: get "How much?" Sixty seconds.
2. Smoke to bridge. Partner throws a common objection. You blow through it and bridge. Three cycles each.
3. Price sheet, last line, then silence. Do not speak until they do.
4. Yes, Address, Close. Partner gives the objection. Repeat until paperwork.
5. Full door to paperwork. Timed. Manager gives two notes. Adjust. Repeat.

Record yourself. Review it. Bring it to your manager. That is how independent reps get better without being babysat.

## 17. Mindset in the field, habits, and the morning

Negativity spreads through a car group. Do not carry it and do not tolerate it. Four steps to mental toughness are in the manual. The working version is simple: knock the next door. Use micro goals. After a bad day, reset, do not narrate. Box breathing is the exercise in the book.

Car groups exist to make everyone better, not to keep each other company. Cooperate. Be on time. Help a teammate who is struggling, then get back to work.

Morning routines are not decoration. Set a wake-up time and do not negotiate it. The Blueprint's daily list, without the hour and door-count targets: wake-up time, morning routine (S.A.V.E.R.S. or C.P.R.), first door picked the night before, stay out, last door pre-decided, role play tonight even if it is ten minutes.

Habits compound. Sleep, food, water, and movement are part of the job. A burned-out body sells like a burned-out pitch.

Customize the pitch once the skeleton is cold. Do not be a robot. Mirror the person in front of you. Approaching people outside is its own skill: be observant, be casual, do not sneak up.
